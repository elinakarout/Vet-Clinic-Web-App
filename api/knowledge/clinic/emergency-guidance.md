# Emergency guidance

**If your pet has any of the signs on this page, treat it as an emergency. Come
straight in during opening hours, or go to your nearest 24-hour emergency
veterinary hospital if we are closed. Do not book a routine appointment and do
not wait to see whether it improves.**

Call us on your way so we can prepare for your arrival.

## Signs that mean go now

### Breathing

- Difficulty breathing, gasping, or breathing with the mouth open in a cat
- Blue, grey, white or very pale gums or tongue
- Continuous choking, or pawing at the mouth
- Non-stop coughing that stops your pet resting

### Collapse and consciousness

- Collapse, fainting, or an inability to stand
- A seizure lasting more than 2–3 minutes, or repeated seizures
- Sudden disorientation, circling, or unresponsiveness
- Sudden weakness or paralysis in the back legs — especially in cats, where it
  is often extremely painful and needs immediate treatment

### Poisoning and toxin ingestion

Do not wait for symptoms, and **never try to make your pet vomit unless a
veterinarian has told you to** — with some substances that causes more harm.
Bring the packaging with you.

Contact us immediately if your pet has eaten, or may have eaten:

- **Chocolate** — toxic to dogs and cats; dark chocolate and cocoa powder are
  the most dangerous. Signs include restlessness, trembling or shaking, vomiting,
  a racing heart and seizures. **Trembling or shaking after eating chocolate is
  an emergency — go in immediately.**
- **Xylitol** (birch sugar), found in sugar-free gum, sweets and some peanut
  butters — causes a life-threatening drop in blood sugar in dogs
- Grapes, raisins, sultanas or currants
- Onions, garlic, leeks or chives in quantity
- Lilies — any part of the plant, and even the pollen, causes fatal kidney
  failure in cats
- Antifreeze (ethylene glycol)
- Rodent poison, slug pellets or insecticide
- Human medication of any kind, including paracetamol and ibuprofen — both are
  poisonous to pets, and paracetamol is rapidly fatal to cats
- Alcohol, cannabis or recreational drugs
- Batteries, or anything containing them

### Abdomen and digestion

- A swollen, hard or distended abdomen, especially with unproductive retching —
  this may be **bloat (GDV)**, which is fatal within hours in deep-chested dogs
  and needs emergency surgery
- Repeated vomiting or diarrhoea over several hours, particularly with blood
- Vomiting in a puppy or kitten, which dehydrates very fast

### Urinary

- **Straining to urinate and producing nothing, especially in a male cat.** A
  urinary blockage is fatal within about 24–48 hours and is one of the most
  time-critical emergencies we see.
- Blood in the urine with obvious pain or repeated squatting

### Injury and bleeding

- Any road traffic accident, fall from height, or crush injury — even if your
  pet seems fine, internal injuries are common
- Bleeding that does not stop after 5 minutes of firm pressure
- A deep wound, a penetrating wound, or a bite from another animal
- A suspected broken bone, or a limb held at an unnatural angle
- An eye injury, a bulging eye, or sudden blindness

### Birth, heat and other

- A pregnant animal straining for more than 30 minutes without producing a
  puppy or kitten, or more than 2 hours between offspring
- Heatstroke — panting heavily, drooling, very red gums, vomiting or collapse
  after heat or exercise. Move to shade, wet with cool (not ice-cold) water and
  come in immediately.
- Snake bite, scorpion sting, or a sting causing facial swelling
- Sudden severe pain — crying out, hiding, refusing to be touched, or a hunched
  posture

## While you are on your way

- Keep your pet warm, quiet and as still as possible.
- Handle an injured animal as little as you can. Pain makes even a gentle pet
  bite, so use a towel or blanket to lift a cat or small dog.
- Do not offer food or water if surgery may be needed.
- Do not give any human medicine.
- Bring the packaging of anything your pet has swallowed, and a sample of any
  vomit or diarrhoea if you can do so safely.

## If you are not sure

Call us. Describing the problem over the phone costs nothing, and we would much
rather tell you it can wait until tomorrow than have you wait at home with an
animal that needed to be seen an hour ago.
